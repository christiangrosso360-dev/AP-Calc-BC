### Rate-limit protection

Your free tier allows 15,000 requests a day but also caps requests per *minute*. One student's
exam is ~23 grading calls, and it was firing them at **54 per minute** — over a typical 30/min cap
by itself, before any second student exists. That is why 8% of my audit requests failed.

Two changes:

1. **A real rate limiter.** It tracks actual send times over a rolling minute and refuses to exceed
   25. Measured at three different model speeds it now holds **22–23 requests/minute regardless of
   latency** — the point being that a fixed delay only works at the speed it was tuned for, and this
   doesn't care. A full exam still grades in 4–22 seconds, so students lose nothing.
2. **Backoff on rate-limit responses.** A "too many requests" reply is now treated as an expected
   condition, not a failure: wait, then ask again, with the cooldown *shared* across every pending

Rate-limited forever → gives up after ~6 seconds, marks the part ungraded rather than zero, and does
not hang. Four parallel calls with two limited → shared cooldown engaged, all four completed.

## The four things that make it different

1. **It grades what you actually wrote.** Nothing else free reads your free-response and tells you
   which rubric points you earned. Each bullet is shown ✓ or ✗ against your answer.
2. **A correct answer with no work earns nothing** — on any part that asks you to justify. That's
   how real readers score, and it's the habit that costs students the most points in May.
3. **Every exam gets a fresh blueprint.** College Board publishes each unit as a *range* — Series
   17–18%, Limits 4–7%, Integration 17–20% — not a fixed number, so no two real exams have the same
   shape. Each exam here samples a new allocation inside those ranges. Over 400 builds that produced
   **147 distinct blueprints, none of them outside a published range.** Most free practice hands you
   a random pile, so the score means much less.
4. **It tells you where you lost it** — per-unit bars, your weakest unit with a link straight to it,
   and a note separating "missing justification" from "missing calculus." The results table now also
   shows how many questions each unit got and how many the blueprint allowed.
5. **Six completely fresh exams in a row.** Questions you've already been served are held back until
   a unit runs low. Before this, a student's first repeated question turned up on their **second**
   sitting; now it's their **seventh**, which is the bank's real capacity. The setup screen shows how
   much of the bank you've worked through, with a "start over" link. Stored in your browser only.
6. **Fewer one-step drills.** 15% of the bank is bare mechanical exercises (Unit 6 alone is 38%), and
   an uncapped exam averaged 7 of 42 — which flatters the student, because the real exam rarely asks
   anything that nakedly. Capped at 10%; surplus drills are swapped for fuller questions from the
   same unit so the blueprint doesn't move.
7. **It shows you the clock.** New section — *The clock* — times every question silently and then
   tells you what pacing cost you: *"You never reached 2 questions. The 2 you overspent on and still
   got wrong cost you 8:16 — about 4 questions' worth of clock."* There's a bar per question against
   your time budget, and the four worst offenders listed by name. **A paper practice test physically
   cannot do this**, and pacing is what actually sinks students in May. This is the stat people
   screenshot.

## A scoring bug worth knowing about (now fixed)

Your free-tier grader occasionally fails on a part — rate limits, a brief outage. When that happened
the part scored **0** but its points still counted in the total, so a network blip on our side
quietly lowered a real student's predicted score, and looked identical to "you did badly."

Two changes: failed parts are now **retried once**, and anything still ungradeable is dropped from
*both* sides of the fraction rather than marked wrong — with a line on the report explaining it.
Tested by forcing a permanent failure on a 3-point part: a perfect paper still scores 108 and a 5,
where before it would have been marked down. This matters more than any feature; a predicted score
you can't trust is worse than no predicted score.

## One thing worth knowing about the blueprint

A whole number of questions can't always land inside a published band. Unit 10's range is 17–18%,
which on a 42-question exam is 7.14 to 7.56 questions — so it gets 7 (16.7%) or 8 (19.0%), and
neither is literally "inside" 17–18%. That's forced by arithmetic and the real exam has exactly the
same problem. It's also why the results table shows **"8 q of 7–8"** rather than a percentage: a
percentage against the band would look wrong even when the exam is right.

For the same reason the half exam is coarser than the full one — with 22 questions, one question is
4.5%, so the bands can only be approximated. If you ever want it tighter, the fix is a longer half.
